Abstract

Flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies are inherently stochastic: different noise seeds can produce action chunks of varying quality from the same observation. At test time, however, the policy typically commits to a single sample without an explicit estimate of its quality. In fine-grained manipulation, executing a suboptimal action can irreversibly disrupt task progress and lead to failure. To exploit policy stochasticity, we introduce a training-free two-axis trigger that uses two initial samples to measure within-chunk temporal variation and cross-sample variance, detecting the critical states. Once triggered, a JEPA-based latent world model ranks candidate action chunks by predicting action-conditioned future latent states and the corresponding changes in task progress. By operating in latent space, the verifier avoids pixel reconstruction and enables efficient and more accurate ranking. Across three real-world tasks (i.e., pen insertion, pipetting, and apple placement), our method raises the average completion score from 50.8% to 65.5%, while adding 326 ms for sampling and verification per triggered decision on a single RTX 4090. Consistent gains are also observed in simulation, where the average success rate rises from 66.4% to 73.6% in the clean setting of RoboTwin 2.0, from 62.8% to 69.6% in its randomized setting, and from 96.9% to 97.6% on LIBERO.

Paradigm comparison

We argue that latent world models are a natural fit in the setting of repeated sampling and verification. The verifier evaluates the quality of action chunk samples by predicting the progress or relative ranking. Therefore, the model must understand world dynamics and representation of the future states resulting from executing the action chunk. In addition, verification is a low-dimensional problem: how much an action chunk advances the task is a scalar judgment, and most camera pixels are irrelevant to it. This motivates verification in a latent space, which compresses the pixel observation into compact and dynamic-rich features, where we can jointly predict the latent states and task progress.

Method

Two-axis trigger

Repeated sampling and verification add computation, so the trigger allocates this cost to states where it is most useful. It is designed to capture two complementary conditions: contact-rich interactions, where manipulation is more complex and failures tend to occur, and uncertain states, where different samples provide meaningful alternatives for the verifier.

Two initial action chunks provide both signals. The temporal axis measures changes between consecutive actions within each chunk, capturing motion variation associated with challenging interactions. The sample axis measures disagreement between the two chunks, capturing policy uncertainty. Each chunk is normalized by its average action magnitude to avoid favoring fast-moving stages.

Latent world model verifier

Action selection requires estimating the consequences of each candidate. A JEPA-based world model predicts action-conditioned future visual latents and task progress in a shared latent space, avoiding the cost of reconstructing future images. Once triggered, the policy expands the pool to 10 candidates, and the verifier selects the action chunk with the largest cumulative predicted progress.

Each action chunk is divided into sub-chunks for multi-step latent prediction. This provides denser supervision than predicting only the final state, while reducing inference cost and error accumulation compared with predicting after every individual action.

Progress estimation also requires task information: the same transition can advance “closing the closet” while reversing “opening the closet.” A task-specific learnable query conditions the progress prediction branch, allowing the verifier to evaluate future states relative to the instructed task.

The attention mask prevents latent state prediction from attending to the task query. Without this restriction, task information could leak into the dynamics model and encourage shortcuts based on task semantics rather than the physical consequences of the action. The progress branch can use task information to judge whether a transition is useful, while the latent prediction branch models the environment dynamics independently of the task. This separation adapts the pretrained world model for verification while preserving its original modeling objective.
